Executive summary

Five key findings in this report.

We put the question to the data. Across the 2026 Singapore survey, employees filed 47,309 responses rating their workplaces on trust, support and performance. Organisations were ranked by a caregiver support score, built from what caregivers themselves experience, and split into low, medium and high support bands. Then we looked at how each band performs.

  1. The question behind this report

    Can an organisation support its caregivers without giving ground on agility, innovation, commitment or service?

  2. 68%

    Caregiving is a mainstream workforce reality.

    68% of employees in the 2026 Singapore survey care for children, elders or both: 35% care for both, 23% for elders only, 10% for children only. Among employees who stated their caregiving status, 86% carry at least one responsibility. The sandwich group, caring for children and elders at once, is the single largest employee segment in the survey.

    2026 Singapore Trust Index™ survey, 47,309 responses.
  3. 64%

    Pressure is unevenly distributed.

    Statutory leave in Singapore now covers early childhood well. Eldercare runs on annual leave and goodwill. In low-support large organisations, elder carers report the weakest willingness to give extra effort in the entire dataset, 64%, and retention intention falls to 67% among employees without caregiving duties.

    2026 Singapore Trust Index™ survey, low-support large organisations.
  4. 12–19 pts

    Support and performance occur together, everywhere we measured.

    In every company size segment in Singapore, high-support organisations outscore low-support organisations on all six outcomes, from agility to retention. In large organisations the gaps run from 12 to 19 percentage points. Nowhere does the expected trade-off appear.

    2026 Singapore Trust Index™ survey. Association, not causation.
  5. 67% → 78%

    Employees without caregiving duties gain too.

    On every measured outcome, Singapore employees with no caring responsibilities report stronger results in high-support organisations than in low-support ones. In large organisations their retention intention rises from 67% to 78%.

    2026 Singapore Trust Index™ survey, large organisations.
  6. 83% vs 72%

    Caregivers report stronger cultural conditions in Best Workplaces™ across every company size.

    Positive responses from caregivers on work-life balance are 83% versus 72% in large companies, 88% versus 74% in medium companies, 90% versus 76% in small companies, and 93% versus 78% in micro companies. In each pair, the first figure is Best Workplaces™ and the second is Rest within the same size group.

    2026 Singapore Trust Index™ survey, caregivers only.
Foreword

This year one cohort stood out.

Every year we ask Singapore’s employees what their workplaces feel like from the inside. This year one cohort stood out. More than two-thirds of the people in our 2026 survey are caring for someone: children, elderly parents, or both at once.

Employers have treated that as a private matter for a long time. The data in this report says it is a workforce reality, and an economic one. Singapore became a super-aged society this year. Family leave has expanded at successive National Day Rallies, from shareable parental leave to bigger childcare leave entitlements, and the care has to land somewhere.

The organisations in these pages offer a practical answer to a hard question: how do you back your caregivers and keep the business sharp? What they show is that support and performance grow from the same roots. Trust. Clear expectations. Managers who plan instead of improvise. That is a finding worth acting on.

Evelyn KwekManaging Director, Great Place To Work® ASEAN & ANZ

What this means

Caregiver support is a talent strategy.

For employers

For employers, the implication is that caregiver support is a talent strategy question with an operational answer: design coverage as a system, equip managers, and audit the leave calendar for eldercare as closely as for childcare.

The organisations that do it show up in this data as stronger on every measure, retention and agility included. A benefits budget alone will not get them there. Culture does the heavier lifting, and culture is a management capability.

What leaders should do
For policymakers

For policymakers, the survey adds a workplace view to a national conversation that has so far centred on entitlements. Statutory leave is expanding; what employees describe at work is everything that happens around the leave.

Coverage, manager behaviour and trust decide whether new entitlements become real support or a fresh source of friction.

Chapter 01

Why this matters now.

Two National Day Rallies set the context.

21.4%

56.6%

At the 2024 Rally, Prime Minister Lawrence Wong announced a new Shared Parental Leave scheme: up to ten weeks for parents to share, fully in force from 1 April 2026 and bringing total paid parental leave to thirty weeks in a child’s first year.1

At the 2026 Rally on 23 August, the expansion continued. Working parents with children aged 12 and below will receive eight to twelve days of childcare leave a year, scaled to the number of children, up from an arrangement that gave parents six days for young children and two for older ones. The Government will fund the full cost of statutory child-related leave.2

At the same time the population crossed a threshold the country had been approaching for years. Singapore is now officially a super-aged society, with 21.4% of citizens aged 65 and above, up from 13.7% a decade ago. By 2035, more than one in four citizens will be 65 or older.3 Someone has to look after them, and increasingly that someone holds a job.

Both adults in most families already do. Dual-income couples made up 56.6% of married couples in 2025, up from 52.5% in 2020.4 When both partners work and one in five citizens is past 65, the arithmetic of care gets unforgiving. Care hours land somewhere, and they land on the workforce.

National policy has largely answered whether employees need support. The live question for employers is how to provide it while staying competitive. That is the question this report takes to the data.

Chapter 02

A mainstream workforce reality.

The 2026 Singapore survey considered 94,373 employees across the national list and collected 47,309 responses. Asked about caregiving, respondents split as follows: 35% care for both children and elders, 23% for elders only, 10% for children only, and 11% for neither. A further 21% left the question blank or gave no response.

Among all respondents, 68% carry at least one caregiving responsibility. Among the 79% who told us their status, the figure is 86%. Either way, caregiving is the majority experience of this workforce, and the sandwich group is its largest single segment. The employee caring for a parent with hospital appointments in the morning and a child’s school event at night is the modal employee in this survey.

55%

of respondents are aged 35 to 54.

31%

hold manager roles, frontline to executive.

The workforce profile reinforces the point. The largest age block, 32%, is 35 to 44, followed by 27% aged 26 to 34 and 23% aged 45 to 54. More than half of respondents sit in the 35-to-54 band where careers peak and care demands stack up.

Two-thirds, 67%, are individual contributors, and 31% hold manager roles from frontline to executive level, the people who absorb both caregiving and coverage decisions.

Chapter 03

The experience of caregivers.

Caregiving pressure is real, and it is not shared evenly.

In low-support large organisations, the weakest cells of the entire dataset cluster: elder carers’ willingness to give extra effort sits at 64%, and retention intention among employees with no caregiving duties falls to 67%.

Every group does better in high-support organisations, where retention intention spans 78% to 88% across the four caregiving groups and extra effort runs 79% to 84%.

In low-support large organisations, sandwich-group employees report the highest retention intention of any group, 77%, against 67% for employees with no responsibilities.

Caregiver voices

Caregiver voices.

“I’m the main caregiver for my elderly mum, and I have been applying annual leave to bring her to doctor appointments.”
Employee, media

The voices behind the numbers are specific. An employee in financial services lists the leaves the organisation already offers, “maternity leave, shared parental leave/childcare leave, marriage leave but unfortunately not caregiver leave.”

An employee in media writes: “I’m the main caregiver for my elderly mum, and I have been applying annual leave to bring her to doctor appointments”, and fears moving to unpaid leave once that runs out.

An employee in professional services describes returning from twenty weeks of approved maternity leave at full allocation, then missing a pay rise for “a lack of recent visibility, which appears to have been influenced by my maternity leave.”

Flexibility without trust surfaces repeatedly. A telecommunications employee describes arrangements that “are not entirely flexible at all”, and questions why taking flexi-family leave should require proof of caring “if the company genuinely intends for it to be part of an employees’ benefits.”

And in frontline transport, a supervisor writes with unusual economy: “Many staffs sacrifice personal and family time due to long working hours.” For shift-based roles, flexibility conversations start from a different place than hybrid office work.

Chapter 04 · The flagship finding

A false trade-off.

“Many employees feel some level of guilt when taking time off because they know their work is not always easy to hand over.”
Employee, healthcare

An employee in healthcare describes the fear: “many employees feel some level of guilt when taking time off because they know their work is not always easy to hand over.” If supporting caregivers meant colleagues silently absorbing their work, that guilt would show up as weaker effort, agility and service in high-support organisations.

It does not. In every size segment, high-support organisations outscore low-support organisations on all six measured outcomes, and in large, medium and small organisations the scores rise consistently across the bands.

Chapter 04 · The false trade-off

Employees without caregiving duties gain too.

Customer service gains 19 points between low- and high-support large organisations, agility 16, extra effort 15, advocacy 15, innovation culture 14, retention intention 12. The two outcomes a skeptic would sacrifice first, agility and innovation, sit among the strongest. Medium organisations repeat the pattern (retention intention 72% to 80% to 88%), and so do small ones (agility 73% to 78% to 94%). Micro organisations are directionally positive but noisy, as thin samples are, so the analysis rests on the larger segments.

The second finding is the one leaders ask about most. Employees with no caregiving duties also do better in high-support organisations: agility 68% to 76% to 84%, retention intention 67% to 69% to 78%, extra effort 66% to 70% to 79% across the bands.

On the measured outcomes, support costs colleagues without caring responsibilities nothing.

Neither = employees with no caregiving responsibilities. Association, not causation.

Two boundaries.

Two boundaries keep this honest. The bands are relative: high support means the top third of the selected sample, and the comparison is association. The outcomes are employee perceptions and intentions, reported experiences rather than revenue lines. Within those boundaries the result is consistent across four segments and six outcomes: where caregivers feel supported, everything else measured is stronger too.

Chapter 05

What the best do differently.

What separates organisations where caregivers thrive? Caregivers in Best Workplaces™ report stronger experiences across all nine cultural conditions in every company-size group. In large companies, 88% say they can take time off when needed, compared with 83% in the Rest group. Trust and autonomy scores are 86% versus 79%, while work-life balance is 83% versus 72%. The largest large-company gap is psychological health: 80% versus 68%.

Chapter 05 · What the best do differently

How do they do it?

The practices behind the numbers share one idea: coverage gets designed before leave begins.

    Managers and teammates “plan ahead, share responsibilities, and stay connected to ensure continuity” when someone takes extended leave.
  • Best Workplaces™ 2026Rank TBCZenika Singapore
    “Project workloads are rebalanced early and supported by internal bench capacity or temporary backfills”, followed by phased return schedules and structured post-leave check-ins.
  • Best Workplaces™ 2026Rank TBCmonday.com Singapore

  • Best Workplaces™ 2026Rank TBCTTS Group

In employees’ words.

A caregiver whose helper did not return from home leave writes that managers “approved two weeks of WFH arrangements to allow me time to find a replacement caregiver for my special needs son”. Another values benefits that let the team “go home on time to take care of our family, even if it means catching up on our work later in the evening/week”.

Chapter 06

What leaders should do.

Three moves follow from the evidence, and all three are operating decisions more than policy decisions. The practical lift is managerial: treating care as part of how work gets done, which is precisely what the data rewards.

  1. 01

    Treat caregiver support as talent strategy.

    When 68% of the workforce carries care responsibilities, support determines who stays. Retention intention is 12 points higher in high-support large organisations, and the leave architecture has an eldercare hole: caregivers are spending annual leave on hospital appointments. Audit the leave calendar for eldercare the way you would audit it for childcare, and measure take-up, because a policy list that runs from maternity to marriage leave and stops short of caregiver leave is a retention risk in waiting.

  2. 02

    Build coverage as a system, not goodwill.

    The guilt an employee feels about burdening colleagues is the real cost of unplanned leave, and every Best Workplace™ in this report plans it away: coverage scheduled before leave starts, cross-training, bench capacity, phased returns. Treat leave coverage like any other operational risk, with named owners and a plan, and the support stops depending on individual kindness.

  3. 03

    Equip managers for flexibility with accountability.

    Selected caregiver comments describe specific manager accommodation during care disruptions. Among caregivers in large companies, trust and autonomy scores are 86% in Best Workplaces™ and 79% in the Rest group. The gap is also present in medium, small and micro companies. Flexibility that demands proof of care reads as negotiation. Train managers to plan coverage and review unnecessary administrative friction while maintaining clear expectations and applicable requirements, then hold them accountable for team outcomes, which is what the high-support organisations in Chapter 4 already do.

How this report was built.

The survey base for the standard comparisons is the Singapore 2026 national list: 94,373 employees considered, 47,309 responses collected, including 25,318 employees and 17,987 responses in Best Workplaces™.

The caregiver support score.

The caregiver support score is built from caregivers’ own experience. For each organisation we calculated the share of caregivers rating 4 or 5 out of 5 on four statements: work-life balance, ability to take time off when needed, management showing interest in them as a person, and special and unique benefits. The support score is the average of those four shares. Organisations were ranked by score and split into roughly equal low, medium and high bands. Bands are relative to the selected sample rather than fixed standards, and boundaries move when filters change.

Outcome scores.

Outcome scores are the share of valid responses rated 4 or 5, covering all employees, with each organisation weighted equally. Six outcomes are used: agility, customer service, extra effort, innovation culture, recruitment and advocacy, and retention intention.

Comparisons by company size.

Caregiver conditions and Best Workplaces™-versus-Rest outcomes are presented by company size. These size-specific results have not been combined into an all-Singapore average.

Employee comments.

Employee comments were collected from two open questions, one on what makes the company a great place to work and one on what employees would change. Of 24,229 submitted responses, 2,748 were non-substantive and 21,481 were analysed. Comments are published anonymously, with industry named only, and preserve original wording.

Evidence limits.

All comparisons show association, and none show causation. Outcomes are employee perceptions and intentions rather than direct measures of business performance. The survey does not measure whether employees without caregiving duties regard leave arrangements as fair. Organisation-level correlation analysis was outside these exports, so the report relies on band comparisons. The micro-organisation support-band subgroup grid remains excluded due to thin samples. The micro conditions comparison in Chapter 5 is a different view and does not resolve the missing sample sizes in that grid. FAQs

Questions about the report.

How many employees in Singapore are caregivers?

In the 2026 Singapore survey, 68% of all respondents care for children, elders or both: 35% care for both, 23% for elders only and 10% for children only. Among employees who stated their caregiving status, 86% carry at least one caregiving responsibility.

Does supporting caregivers hurt business performance?

Not on the outcomes measured. In every company size segment, high-support organisations outscore low-support organisations on all six outcomes: agility, customer service, extra effort, innovation culture, recruitment and advocacy, and retention intention. In large organisations the gaps run from 12 to 19 percentage points. The outcomes are employee perceptions and intentions, and the comparison shows association, not causation.

Do employees without caregiving duties lose out?

No. In large organisations, employees with no caregiving duties report stronger results in high-support organisations than in low-support ones: agility rises from 68% to 76% to 84%, retention intention from 67% to 69% to 78%, and extra effort from 66% to 70% to 79% across the bands.

How is the caregiver support score calculated?

For each organisation, Great Place To Work calculated the share of caregivers rating 4 or 5 out of 5 on four statements: work-life balance, ability to take time off when needed, management showing interest in them as a person, and special and unique benefits. The support score is the average of those four shares. Organisations were ranked and split into roughly equal low, medium and high bands.

What do the Best Workplaces do differently for caregivers?

Coverage gets designed before leave begins: managers plan handovers, workloads are rebalanced early, bench capacity or temporary backfills cover gaps, and returns are phased. Caregivers in Best Workplaces report stronger experiences across all nine cultural conditions measured, in every company-size group.
